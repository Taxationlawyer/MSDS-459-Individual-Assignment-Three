## Part Two

Chapter Seven of the book entitled “Knowledge Graphs,” by Kejriwal, Mayank et al., continues the discussion of information extraction (“IE”). IE can be done using Open IE  (Open Information Extraction)—extracts information by recognizing patterns. Additionally, most of the Open methods consist of four main parts. Moreover, Open IE can be used to extract information from social media (e.g., Twitter, now named X) and blogs. 

(1) Open IE works by manually setting extraction patterns. Open IE uses a classifier to extract “relevant” information, especially relations. Additionally, the classifier assigns a number (a probability) that represents the confidence of the relevance of the extracted information. MinIE (a kind of Open IE) can be adjusted by using “different precision/recall trade-offs.” This is an adjustment that is straight out of statistical learning (and/or machine learning). 

(2) Most of the Open IE methods consist of four parts. The extractor develops a set of extraction rules from general templates. Thereafter, the search engine interface automatically creates extraction rules based on the extraction rules. The assessor estimates whether the extractors output are “relevant.” The fourth component is the database—stores the data. 

(3) The aforementioned systems can be used to mine the World Wide Web, including X (formerly known as Twitter), blogs, and other sources. It is worth restating what is said in Chapter Seven; traditional natural language processing methods do not perform well on social media platforms because (in short) the said platforms have their own jargon that is substantially different from the “traditional” English language.

In conclusion, based on the above discussed statements, web scraping is strongly leveraging the capabilities of statistical learning and/or machine learning as evidenced by the use of classification systems and algorithms and devices that very closely resemble support vector machines as witnessed by the use of hypernodes and hypergraphs, the equivalence of tuples and hyperplanes, respectively, in support vector machines (as mentioned in the Chapter Twelve discussion).
